A belt-driven vane pump supplying pressurised fluid to a hydraulically assisted steering rack. Now largely superseded, but still fitted where high steering loads and simplicity matter.

## What it does

It provides assistance force. Fluid is pumped continuously to a control valve at the rack; as the driver turns, the valve directs pressure to one side of a piston on the rack, adding force to the driver's effort.

The word "continuously" is the design's weakness. The pump runs whenever the engine runs, whether or not any steering is happening, and the surplus flow is simply dumped back to the reservoir through a relief valve. That parasitic loss is real — a few percent of fuel consumption — and it is the main reason electric assistance replaced it.

Its advantages are genuine, though: very high assistance forces are easy to generate, which suits heavily loaded vehicles, and the hydraulic path gives a naturally progressive, well-damped feel that early electric systems struggled to match.

Assistance is speed-sensitive on better systems, using a valve that reduces flow at speed so the steering firms up.

## How it fails

Bearing and vane wear giving a whine that rises with engine speed, and seal leaks. Low fluid causes aeration, which is noisy and destroys the pump quickly.

## How it is serviced

Fluid level and belt condition checked at service.
