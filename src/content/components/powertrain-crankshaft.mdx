The crankshaft is the axis the whole engine is organised around. It is a forged or cast steel shaft with offset throws, and it converts the pistons' reciprocating motion into the rotation that eventually reaches the wheels.

## What it does

Each connecting rod pushes on a throw offset from the shaft's centreline. The distance of that offset is the crank throw, and twice it is the stroke. A longer throw means more torque for the same cylinder pressure and a slower-revving engine, which is one of the reasons a diesel and a petrol engine of the same capacity feel so different.

The shaft runs in plain bearings, not rollers. The <Part id="lubrication.main_bearing" /> does not actually touch the journal in normal running: <Part id="lubrication.oil_pump" /> pressure builds a wedge of oil that the shaft rides on. Lose that pressure and the two metals meet within seconds.

At one end the crank drives the <Part id="powertrain.timing_chain" />. At the other, the <Part id="powertrain.flywheel" /> carries its inertia between power strokes and gives the starter something to turn.

## How it fails

Bearing failure from oil starvation or contamination is by far the most common path, and it usually announces itself as a deep knock that gets worse under load. Actual crank breakage is rare outside modified engines.

## How it is serviced

Nothing routine. Keep the oil clean and at pressure and the crankshaft outlives the car.
