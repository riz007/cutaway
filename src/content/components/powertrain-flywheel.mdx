A heavy steel disc bolted to the back of the crankshaft. Its only trick is inertia, and that inertia is what makes a four-stroke engine usable at all.

## What it does

An engine does not deliver power smoothly. In a four-cylinder, each cylinder fires once every two revolutions, so torque arrives as a series of kicks with gaps between them. The flywheel stores energy during each power stroke and gives it back during the gaps, carrying the crank through compression on the next cylinder. Without it the engine would stall below a few hundred rpm.

Its mass is a trade-off: heavier means a smoother idle and easier pull-away, lighter means the engine revs more freely.

Its rim carries the starter ring gear that the <Part id="electrical.starter_motor" /> engages, and its face is the friction surface the <Part id="drivetrain.clutch" /> clamps against.

Dual-mass flywheels split the disc in two with springs between, isolating gearbox rattle at the cost of being a wearing part.

## How it fails

Heat-checked or scored friction faces after a worn clutch, worn ring gear teeth, and on dual-mass units, worn springs that give a rattle at idle.

## How it is serviced

Inspected or resurfaced whenever the clutch is replaced.
