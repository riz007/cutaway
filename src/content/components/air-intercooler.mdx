A heat exchanger between the <Part id="powertrain.turbocharger" /> and the engine, cooling the air the turbo has just compressed.

## What it does

Compressing air heats it — unavoidably, by the physics of compression. Air leaving a turbo can be over 150 degrees Celsius, and hot air is less dense, which directly undoes some of what the turbo achieved. Cooling the charge back down recovers that density: more oxygen per cylinder filling, so more fuel can be burnt.

The second benefit matters more on petrol engines. Cooler intake charge means lower temperature at the end of compression, which means less tendency to knock, which lets the ECU run more ignition advance and higher boost. Much of a turbo petrol engine's usable output depends on charge cooling.

Most are air-to-air, mounted in the airstream. Water-to-air units are more compact and respond faster.

<Market region="Thailand">
Ambient temperature sets the floor on how cool the charge can get. In 35-degree conditions an intercooler that performs well in Europe leaves the charge much hotter, which is part of why turbocharged engines feel softer in tropical heat and why a blocked or leaf-clogged core is more damaging here.
</Market>

## How it fails

Stone damage, split end tanks, and oil accumulation from a failing turbo.

## How it is serviced

Kept clear of debris. Not a replacement item.
