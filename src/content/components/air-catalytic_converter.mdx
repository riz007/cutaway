A catalytic converter is a ceramic honeycomb coated with platinum, palladium and rhodium. Exhaust gas passes through it and comes out chemically different, without the converter itself being consumed.

## What it does

Three reactions happen at once, which is why it is called a three-way catalyst: carbon monoxide is oxidised to carbon dioxide, unburnt hydrocarbons are oxidised to carbon dioxide and water, and oxides of nitrogen are reduced back to nitrogen.

Those reactions pull in opposite directions — two need oxygen, one needs its absence. They can only all proceed if the mixture is held very close to stoichiometric. That is the real reason for the <Part id="air.o2_sensor" /> and the closed-loop fuelling behind it: not to save fuel, but to keep the catalyst in the narrow window where it works.

The catalyst also needs to be hot, around 400 degrees Celsius, before it does anything. Most of a modern car's tailpipe emissions occur in the first ninety seconds after a cold start, which is why converters are mounted close to the engine.

<Warning>
Runs extremely hot in normal use and hotter still during a misfire. Hot enough to ignite dry grass beneath a parked car.
</Warning>

## How it fails

Poisoning by oil or coolant entering the exhaust, and physical destruction of the honeycomb by raw fuel from a persistent misfire. Both are secondary failures — the converter is the victim, and replacing it without fixing the cause simply destroys the new one.

## How it is serviced

Not a service item. Its efficiency is monitored continuously by a second oxygen sensor downstream.
