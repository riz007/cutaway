A diesel has no spark. It relies on the air in the cylinder becoming hot enough through compression alone to ignite injected fuel. When the engine is cold, that is not guaranteed, and the glow plug makes up the difference.

## What it does

An electrically heated element projects into each combustion chamber or pre-chamber and reaches well over 800 degrees Celsius within a couple of seconds. It gives the injected spray a hot spot to ignite against while the cylinder walls are still cold and stealing heat from the compression.

It also keeps running for a while after the engine starts — post-glow — which smooths the notoriously rough first minute of a cold diesel and cuts the white smoke of partly burnt fuel.

<Market region="Thailand">
Thailand's climate means glow plugs do far less work than in temperate markets, so failures often go unnoticed for a long time. They still matter on cold upcountry mornings and are worth checking when a diesel becomes hard to start or smokes on start-up.
</Market>

## How it fails

The element burns out or swells in the head, making removal difficult. A single failed plug on a modern engine usually just sets a code; several make cold starting genuinely hard.

## How it is serviced

Replaced when faulty, usually as a set, and tested by resistance.
