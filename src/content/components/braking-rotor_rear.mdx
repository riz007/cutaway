Usually a solid disc rather than a ventilated one, and thinner than the front, because the rear axle has far less energy to dissipate.

## What it does

The same job as the front rotor, at roughly a third of the workload. Under heavy braking weight transfers forward, unloading the rear tyres and limiting how much braking they can take before locking. Sizing the rear rotor for the same duty as the front would add unsprung mass for nothing.

Because it runs cooler, it does not need internal vanes, and because it is lighter it costs less unsprung weight at the axle that most affects ride.

On many rear discs the centre section also forms a small drum for a parking brake shoe, giving the mechanical hold without complicating the caliper.

## How it fails

Wear below minimum thickness, corrosion pitting from light use, and scoring where a pad has worn through to the backing plate.

## How it is serviced

Measured at each pad change and replaced in axle pairs.
