A torsion spring across the vehicle, connected to both wheels of an axle by drop links. It is one of the few components that resists one motion while ignoring another.

## What it does

When both wheels rise together — over a crest, or on a smooth road — the bar simply rotates in its bushes and does nothing. When the wheels move in opposite directions, as they do when the body rolls in a corner, the bar has to twist, and it resists.

That selectivity is the point. It reduces body roll without stiffening the ride, which a stiffer spring would inevitably do.

It also has a second, more subtle use: front-to-rear balance. A stiffer bar on one axle transfers more load across that axle in a corner, and a more heavily loaded pair of tyres gives up grip sooner. Stiffening the front bar therefore adds understeer; stiffening the rear adds oversteer. Bar sizing is one of the primary tools for tuning a car's handling balance.

## How it fails

Worn drop-link ball joints, which is the classic source of a light rattle or knock over small bumps at low speed, and perished mounting bushes.

## How it is serviced

Links and bushes inspected. Both are inexpensive and commonly replaced.
