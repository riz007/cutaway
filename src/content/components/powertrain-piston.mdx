A piston is a light aluminium slug that seals the bottom of the combustion chamber and turns gas pressure into mechanical force. In a running engine at 3,000 rpm it stops and reverses direction fifty times a second.

## What it does

On the power stroke, burning gas above the piston can reach fifty times atmospheric pressure. That pressure acts on the piston crown and pushes it down the bore. The force passes through the gudgeon pin into the <Part id="powertrain.connecting_rod" />, and because the rod is attached to an offset throw on the <Part id="powertrain.crankshaft" />, straight-line push becomes rotation.

The piston does not touch the bore directly. The <Part id="powertrain.piston_rings" /> seal against the wall, and a thin film of oil separates ring from iron. The piston's skirt only stops it rocking.

<Note>
The relationship between crank angle and piston height is pure geometry — a slider-crank — which is why the animation here is exact rather than approximate. The piston is not at its half-way point when the crank is at ninety degrees.
</Note>

## How it fails

Detonation, where the charge explodes rather than burns, hammers the crown and can break the ring lands. Prolonged overheating can seize a piston in its bore. Both are usually consequences of poor fuel, a lean mixture, or a cooling fault, not of the piston itself.

## How it is serviced

Pistons are replaced only when the engine is stripped. Their condition is read as evidence: a scuffed skirt, a burnt crown or a broken ring land each point to a different underlying cause.
