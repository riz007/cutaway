Stacked curved steel strips clamped together, bolted to the chassis at each end with the axle sitting on top. The oldest suspension design still in mainstream production, and still the right answer for a load-carrier.

## What it does

Uniquely among springs, it also locates the axle. A coil spring only pushes; a leaf spring resists fore-aft and lateral movement as well, so a leaf-sprung axle needs no trailing arms, no Panhard rod and no extra bushes. For a pickup that has to be cheap, strong and easy to repair, that is compelling.

It is naturally progressive. As it flattens under load, its effective length shortens and its rate rises, so it can be tolerable unladen and still support a tonne in the bed. Helper leaves that only engage under heavy load extend this further.

Friction between the leaves provides a small amount of self-damping, which is also why an old leaf spring can feel harsh — the leaves stick rather than slide until the force is enough to break them free.

## How it fails

Broken leaves, sagging with age, and worn shackle bushes and pins giving a knock.

## How it is serviced

Inspected for cracks, sag and worn shackles.
