An electric fan behind the <Part id="cooling.radiator" />, pulling air through the core when the vehicle is not moving fast enough to do it on its own.

## What it does

At road speed, ram air through the grille does all the cooling needed. In traffic there is no ram air, and that is exactly when the engine is making heat with no airflow to shed it. The fan covers that gap.

It is controlled by coolant temperature and by air-conditioning demand — switch the A/C on and the fan usually runs regardless of engine temperature, because the <Part id="hvac.condenser" /> sitting ahead of the radiator needs airflow too.

Older engines used a belt-driven fan with a viscous clutch, which wasted power whenever it was not needed. Electric fans decoupled fan speed from engine speed, and modern units are variable rather than simply on or off, which is quieter and draws less current.

In sustained 35-degree traffic the fan is doing continuous work rather than intermittent duty. A failed fan may cause no problem at all on the open road and overheat the engine within minutes in city traffic — a distinctive symptom pattern worth recognising.
</Market>

## How it fails

Motor bearing failure, and failure of the relay or control module. The fan is a large 12 V load, so wiring and connectors run hot.

## How it is serviced

Kept clear of debris. Its operation should be verified whenever overheating is investigated.
