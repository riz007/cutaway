A butterfly valve in a bore between the intake duct and the manifold. It is how a petrol engine's power is controlled, and it is a slightly absurd way to do it.

## What it does

Closing the butterfly restricts airflow, so less air enters, so less fuel is matched to it, so less power is made. The engine spends most of its life sucking against a partly closed valve, and that pumping work is a large part of why petrol engines are inefficient at light load. A diesel has no throttle at all — it controls power by fuel quantity alone — which is much of why it is more efficient in traffic.

Modern throttles are drive-by-wire: the pedal is a sensor, and a motor positions the butterfly. That decoupling is what makes cruise control, stability control and hybrid torque blending possible, since the ECU can command any throttle position independently of the driver's foot.

## How it fails

Carbon builds up around the butterfly edge and the bore, changing the airflow at small openings and causing a hunting or stalling idle. Position sensor and motor faults put the vehicle into a reduced-power limp mode.

## How it is serviced

Cleaned when idle quality deteriorates. Many vehicles need a throttle relearn afterwards.
