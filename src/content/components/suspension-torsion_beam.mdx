Two trailing arms joined by a cross-member, and the reason so many small cars have a large boot.

## What it does

It is a partly independent design that occupies almost no space. The cross-member is deliberately open in section — a U or V rather than a closed tube — so it is stiff in bending but relatively easy to twist.

That twisting is the whole trick. When one wheel rises over a bump, the beam twists, letting that wheel move with only a modest effect on the other. The same twisting resistance also acts as an anti-roll bar, so no separate one is needed.

Its position matters too: mounting the beam ahead of the wheel centres gives the assembly a degree of passive rear steer that improves cornering stability.

The compromises are honest. Camber control is worse than a multilink, and the two wheels are not truly independent. In exchange, it is light, cheap, compact and reliable, and it intrudes so little that the fuel tank and spare can sit in the space a multilink would occupy.

## How it fails

Bush wear at the trailing arm pivots, giving vague handling and uneven rear tyre wear. Corrosion of the beam itself on older vehicles.

## How it is serviced

Bushes inspected. Rear alignment is usually non-adjustable.
