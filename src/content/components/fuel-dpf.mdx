A honeycomb of porous ceramic with alternate channels blocked, so exhaust gas is forced through the walls. Soot stays on the surface; clean gas passes.

## What it does

Diesel combustion happens in a fuel-rich core where there is not enough oxygen to burn all the carbon, so it inevitably makes soot. The DPF traps upwards of 95 percent of it.

A filter that only traps would block, so it periodically burns its load off. Passive regeneration happens whenever exhaust temperature is naturally high enough. When it is not, the ECU forces an active regeneration: it injects extra fuel late in the cycle, raising exhaust temperature to around 600 degrees Celsius and oxidising the soot to CO2. Ash from oil additives does not burn and slowly accumulates, which sets the filter's ultimate life.

<Market region="Thailand">
Euro 5 became the standard for diesel vehicles in Thailand on 1 January 2024, which makes DPFs standard equipment on new diesels rather than an export-market curiosity. City-only use is their enemy: a pickup that never sees a sustained run may never complete a regeneration and will eventually go into limp mode.
</Market>

<Warning>
Reaches roughly 600 degrees Celsius during regeneration. Do not park over dry grass during or just after one.
</Warning>

## How it fails

Blocking, usually from interrupted regenerations.

## How it is serviced

Not a routine item. Let a signalled regeneration finish.
