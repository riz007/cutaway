Without assistance, stopping a car hard would take a leg press. The booster multiplies the driver's pedal force by roughly four before it reaches the master cylinder.

## What it does

A conventional booster is a large diaphragm with engine vacuum on both sides. Pressing the pedal opens a valve that admits atmospheric pressure to the rear chamber, and the pressure difference across a large area produces a large force. It is not the vacuum doing the work so much as the atmosphere pushing.

Diesel engines throttle very little and produce poor manifold vacuum, so they use a dedicated vacuum pump. Hybrids and electric vehicles have no reliable vacuum at all — the engine may be off — so they use an electrically driven booster, which has the useful side effect of being able to build pressure faster than a human, and independently of the pedal. That is a prerequisite for automatic emergency braking.

<Note>
There is a reserve of vacuum stored in the booster. If the engine stops, you have roughly one or two assisted applications before the pedal becomes very hard. It has not failed — the assistance has simply run out.
</Note>

## How it fails

A perished diaphragm or a failed check valve loses assistance, leaving a hard pedal and long stopping distances. A leaking booster can also draw brake fluid or unmetered air, causing an unrelated-looking running fault.

## How it is serviced

Not a service item. The check valve and hose are worth inspecting when the pedal feel changes.
