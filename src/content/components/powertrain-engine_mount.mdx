Three or four rubber-and-steel mounts carry the entire powertrain and are the only thing standing between its vibration and the cabin.

## What it does

Two conflicting jobs. It has to be soft, so combustion pulses and idle shake do not reach the body. It also has to be stiff, so the engine does not rock when you accelerate hard or lift off — a transverse engine tries to twist about its crank axis under torque, and one mount, usually a torque strut, takes most of that.

Hydraulic mounts add a fluid chamber tuned to damp the specific low frequency of an idling engine, and active mounts add a solenoid that changes the rate depending on engine speed.

## How it fails

The rubber perishes and cracks with heat and age, or a hydraulic mount splits and weeps its fluid. The symptoms are a thump on gear engagement, vibration through the floor at idle, and an engine that visibly rocks when the throttle is blipped.

Sustained heat and stop-start traffic age mount rubber faster than in temperate climates, so a mount that would last a decade elsewhere may not here.

## How it is serviced

Inspected visually. Replaced when the rubber has separated or the mount has collapsed.
