Two long rails joined by cross-members, with the body, powertrain and suspension all bolted to it. The body carries no structural load.

## What it does

It separates structure from bodywork, and that separation is the entire argument for it. The frame can be built for strength and torsional flexibility while the body above simply rides along, which means a pickup can be built as a chassis cab, a double cab or a flatbed on the same underpinnings, and a damaged body can be replaced without touching the structure.

Its flexibility is deliberate, not a shortcoming. A ladder frame twists more than a monocoque, and on very uneven ground that compliance lets the wheels follow the terrain instead of loading the structure. For towing and load carrying it also concentrates loads into two strong rails rather than distributing them through sheet metal.

The costs are weight, a high floor and a high centre of gravity, and worse on-road handling and refinement — body mount rubbers isolate noise but also allow the body to move relative to the frame.

## How it fails

Corrosion inside the rails, cracking around welds and mounting brackets on heavily worked vehicles, and perished body mount bushings.

## How it is serviced

Inspected for corrosion and cracking; body mounts checked for collapse.
