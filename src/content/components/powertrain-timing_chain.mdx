The timing chain locks the camshafts in fixed phase with the crankshaft. Everything about valve timing depends on that relationship being exact and staying exact.

## What it does

The camshafts must turn at exactly half crank speed, because a four-stroke cycle takes two crankshaft revolutions. The chain enforces both the ratio and the phase: not just how fast the cams turn, but precisely where they are relative to each piston.

A hydraulic tensioner, fed by <Part id="lubrication.oil_pump" /> pressure, keeps the slack side taut. Plastic guides stop the chain flogging against the casting.

<Note>
Get the phase wrong by a few teeth on an interference engine and the valves and pistons occupy the same space at the same moment. That is the difference between a chain job and a new cylinder head.
</Note>

## How it fails

Chains stretch as their pins and rollers wear, which retards cam timing and shows up as a rough idle, a rattle on cold start before oil pressure builds, and often a correlation fault code. Worn plastic guides can shatter. Infrequent oil changes accelerate all of it, because a chain is lubricated by the same oil that is carrying the engine's debris.

## How it is serviced

Chains are nominally lifetime parts, but "lifetime" assumes the maintenance schedule was followed. Treat a cold-start rattle as a symptom to investigate rather than a quirk. Replacement means opening the front of the engine, so it is not a small job.
