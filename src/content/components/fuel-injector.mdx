An injector is a fast, precise solenoid valve. Its whole purpose is to open for an exact number of milliseconds and atomise fuel into a fine, well-distributed spray.

## What it does

The <Part id="fuel.fuel_rail" /> holds fuel at a known pressure. With pressure fixed, the mass of fuel delivered depends only on how long the valve is open, so the <Part id="electrical.ecu_engine" /> controls fuelling by controlling pulse width — often as short as one and a half milliseconds at idle.

The nozzle matters as much as the timing. Fuel has to be broken into droplets small enough to evaporate and mix with air in the fraction of a second available. Poor atomisation means unburnt fuel, which means wasted energy and a hard life for the <Part id="air.catalytic_converter" />.

Direct-injection engines put the injector in the combustion chamber itself at pressures over 200 bar, which allows charge cooling and stratified running but exposes the nozzle tip to combustion.

## How it fails

Deposits on the nozzle distort the spray pattern long before they block it, which shows up as a rough idle and a lean misfire on one cylinder. Electrical failure of the winding is less common and more obvious.

## How it is serviced

Not routine. Clean fuel and a maintained <Part id="fuel.fuel_filter" /> are the real service. Injectors can be ultrasonically cleaned and flow-tested when a cylinder-specific fault is confirmed.
