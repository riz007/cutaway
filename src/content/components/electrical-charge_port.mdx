The vehicle's inlet, combining AC pins, DC pins, a locking mechanism, temperature sensors and a communication link in one connector.

## What it does

Far more than pass current. Before any power flows, the vehicle and the charging equipment hold a conversation over dedicated pilot pins: the equipment declares how much current it can supply, the vehicle states what it wants, and only when both agree do the contactors close. A charging cable lying in a puddle is inert until that handshake completes.

The connector locks during charging, both so it cannot be pulled out under load — which would arc badly at DC currents — and so it cannot be unplugged by a passer-by.

Temperature sensors sit in the DC pins. At 200 kW the contact resistance of a slightly dirty pin generates real heat, so the vehicle monitors pin temperature and reduces current rather than risk damage. A charge that tapers on a hot day is often this, not the pack.

<Market region="Thailand">
CCS2 is the prevailing DC standard for new vehicles in Thailand, though GB/T appears on some Chinese-market imports. Ambient heat means pin-temperature derating is more commonly encountered here than in cooler climates.
</Market>

## How it fails

Worn or corroded pins raising contact resistance, and lock actuator failure.

## How it is serviced

Kept clean and dry; the flap seal is worth checking.
