Gearbox and final drive combined in a single casing, bolted directly to the end of a transverse engine. It is the layout that defines the modern front-wheel-drive car.

## What it does

It does everything a separate gearbox, propshaft and rear axle would do, in one unit beside the engine. Torque goes in from the clutch or torque converter, through the gearsets, into the <Part id="drivetrain.differential_front" />, and straight out to the driveshafts.

The packaging advantage is enormous and is the reason almost every mainstream car is built this way. There is no propshaft, so no transmission tunnel eating cabin space; the whole powertrain is one subassembly that drops in from below; and the weight sits over the driven wheels, which helps traction.

The costs are real too: the driven wheels also steer, so torque steer and traction limits under power are inherent, and access for repair is poor because everything is packed into the space between the wheel arches.

Continuously variable and dual-clutch transmissions are both packaged this way.

## How it fails

Bearing wear giving a whine that varies with road speed rather than engine speed, and synchroniser wear on manuals causing a crunch on a fast change.

## How it is serviced

Fluid changed on the manufacturer's schedule with the exact specified fluid.
