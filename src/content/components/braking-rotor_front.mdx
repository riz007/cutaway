A brake rotor's real job is not friction — the pads supply that. Its job is to absorb an enormous amount of energy in a few seconds and then get rid of it before the next stop.

## What it does

Stopping a 1,500 kg car from 100 km/h dissipates a bit over half a megajoule. Almost all of it ends up as heat in the rotors, which can climb several hundred degrees during one hard stop.

Front rotors are ventilated: two friction faces separated by curved internal vanes that pump air outward as the wheel turns. That internal airflow, not the exposed surface, does most of the cooling. Cast iron is used because it is cheap, stable, and has a high specific heat — it stores heat well while it waits to shed it.

<Note>
Run the braking scenario and watch the rotor temperature. Repeated stops raise it faster than the airflow removes it. Once the pad material starts to outgas at the interface, braking force falls for the same pedal pressure. That is brake fade, and it is a thermal problem, not a hydraulic one.
</Note>

<Warning>
Reaches temperatures that will cause immediate burns and can remain hot long after the vehicle has stopped.
</Warning>

## How it fails

Wear below minimum thickness, thickness variation from uneven pad deposits — felt as a pulsing pedal — and cracking from severe thermal cycling.

## How it is serviced

Measured at every pad change and replaced in axle pairs once below the minimum thickness cast into the hub.
