Drum brakes look like an anachronism next to discs, and on a rear axle that carries little braking load they remain a perfectly sensible engineering answer.

## What it does

A wheel cylinder pushes two curved shoes outward against the inside of a rotating drum. The geometry gives them a property discs do not have: self-energisation. The leading shoe is dragged by the rotating drum in the direction that presses it harder against the surface, so the brake amplifies its own application. That means useful torque from modest hydraulic pressure.

They also enclose a parking brake mechanism neatly, and being sealed, they resist the corrosion that plagues a lightly used rear disc.

The costs are real: enclosed means poor cooling, so they fade under sustained use, and self-energisation makes their output less linear and more sensitive to friction changes than a disc. On a rear axle that handles maybe thirty percent of the braking, neither cost bites.

## How it fails

Contamination from a leaking wheel cylinder or axle seal, glazed linings, and seized automatic adjusters that let the pedal go long.

## How it is serviced

Inspected at the same interval as pads. The self-adjuster and the parking brake mechanism are cleaned and lubricated; the drum's inner diameter is measured against a maximum cast into the drum.
