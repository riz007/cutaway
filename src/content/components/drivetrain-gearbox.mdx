A set of gear pairs that trade speed for torque, mounted longitudinally behind the engine on a rear- or four-wheel-drive vehicle.

## What it does

An engine makes useful torque over a narrow band — perhaps 1,500 to 4,500 rpm — while the wheels need to turn from zero to motorway speed. The gearbox reconciles the two. First gear multiplies engine torque several times over to get a heavy vehicle moving; top gear is often an overdrive, turning the output faster than the input so the engine can loaf at cruise.

Torque and speed trade exactly: multiply torque by three and you divide speed by three. Nothing is created, only exchanged.

In a manual, all gear pairs are permanently meshed and running; selecting a gear locks one of them to the output shaft through a synchroniser, which is a small cone clutch that matches speeds before the dog teeth engage. In a torque-converter automatic, planetary sets and clutch packs do the same job without interrupting drive.

## How it fails

Synchroniser wear, bearing wear and worn selector detents. On automatics, degraded fluid causes harsh or slipping shifts.

## How it is serviced

Fluid on schedule. Burnt-smelling automatic fluid is an early warning worth acting on.
