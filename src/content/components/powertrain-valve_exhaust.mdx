The hottest moving part in the engine. It sits directly in the path of gas leaving the combustion chamber and can glow visibly under sustained load.

## What it does

It releases burnt gas into the exhaust port. It is smaller than the intake valve, partly because pressurised gas leaves more willingly than atmospheric air arrives, and partly because a smaller head is easier to keep cool.

Cooling is the design problem. The valve has only two paths to lose heat: through the stem into the guide, and — for most of its heat — through the seat face into the seat ring each time it closes. That is why a valve that no longer seats properly overheats quickly: it loses its main cooling path exactly when it is being burnt.

Highly loaded engines use sodium-filled stems, where liquid sodium sloshing inside carries heat up the stem.

<Warning>
Exhaust valves and their ports remain hot enough to burn long after shutdown.
</Warning>

## How it fails

Burning, where a small leak lets hot gas cut a channel across the seat face. Compression falls on that cylinder and the misfire threatens the <Part id="air.catalytic_converter" />.

## How it is serviced

Clearance checks on interval; a burnt valve means head removal.
