The caliper is the clamp. Hydraulic pressure arrives, a piston pushes, and the pads squeeze the rotor from both sides.

## What it does

In a sliding caliper — the common design — there is one piston on the inboard side. It pushes the inner pad against the rotor, and the reaction force slides the caliper body on its guide pins, pulling the outer pad in. Both pads end up clamped with equal force from a single piston.

Force is pressure times piston area. At 100 bar with a 57 mm piston, that is roughly 25 kN of clamp, and the friction it generates acts at the effective radius of the rotor to produce braking torque.

The front axle does most of the work — typically around 70 percent — because weight transfers forward under deceleration, which is why front calipers and rotors are larger.

<Note>
A caliper piston retracts by only a few hundredths of a millimetre when you release the pedal, pulled back by the square-section seal deforming and springing. Pads never fully leave the rotor.
</Note>

## How it fails

Seized guide pins, from dried grease and torn boots, stop the caliper sliding, so only the inner pad wears. A seized piston drags continuously and cooks the pad and rotor. Both are corrosion problems more than wear problems.

## How it is serviced

Guide pins cleaned and re-greased at every pad change; boots and dust seals inspected. Fluid changed every two years.
