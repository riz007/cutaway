One of four or five separate links controlling a single rear wheel. Each link controls one aspect of the wheel's movement, and together they give the designer near-complete freedom.

## What it does

A rigid body has six degrees of freedom. The wheel needs one — vertical travel — and the suspension must remove the other five. A multilink does this with individual links rather than a single arm, and because each link's length and mounting points can be chosen independently, camber, toe and the wheel's fore-aft path can be tuned almost separately from one another.

The most valuable outcome is controlled toe under load. A well-designed multilink makes the outside rear wheel toe in slightly as it is loaded in a corner, which stabilises the car exactly when stability is wanted. Achieving that passively, through geometry alone, is something a torsion beam cannot do.

The price is cost, weight, packaging space, and a lot of bushes and ball joints to wear out. It also intrudes into the boot floor, which is why it appears on larger cars and on EVs where the flat floor is set by the pack anyway.

## How it fails

Bush and joint wear, giving unstable handling and rear tyre wear.

## How it is serviced

Inspected for play. Rear alignment is adjustable and should be checked after any link is replaced.
