The master cylinder is where mechanical force becomes hydraulic pressure. Everything downstream of it — lines, modulator, calipers — is just a way of delivering that pressure to four corners at once.

## What it does

The pushrod from the <Part id="braking.booster" /> drives a piston into a bore full of fluid. Because fluid does not compress, the force divided by the piston area gives a pressure that appears everywhere in the circuit simultaneously. A modest pedal effort becomes well over a hundred bar at the calipers.

Almost every master cylinder is a tandem design: two pistons in one bore, feeding two independent circuits, usually split diagonally so that a failure of either still leaves one front brake working. The pedal travels further and the car stops less well, but it stops.

<Note>
Pressure is force divided by area. A smaller master cylinder bore gives higher pressure for the same pedal force, but requires more pedal travel to move the same volume of fluid. Every brake system is a choice on that trade-off.
</Note>

## How it fails

Internal seals wear and let fluid past the piston rather than pushing it. The symptom is distinctive: a pedal that sinks slowly to the floor under steady foot pressure, with no external leak and no drop in reservoir level.

## How it is serviced

Brake fluid is replaced roughly every two years, because it is hygroscopic and absorbed water lowers its boiling point — which is how a pedal goes soft after a long descent.
