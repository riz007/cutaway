The block is the single largest casting in a combustion engine, and almost everything else hangs off it. Inside are the cylinder bores the pistons run in, the main bearing saddles the crankshaft sits in, and a network of cast passages — the water jacket — that carries coolant around the hottest parts.

## What it does

It resists the loads of combustion. Peak cylinder pressure pushes the piston down hard, and that force has to be reacted somewhere: it travels through the <Part id="powertrain.connecting_rod" /> into the <Part id="powertrain.crankshaft" />, and from the crank's main bearings into the block itself. The block also holds the bores round and parallel while the metal around them expands unevenly with heat, which is why it is far thicker and more ribbed than the pressure alone would suggest.

Most modern blocks are aluminium with iron cylinder liners, a compromise that saves weight while keeping a hard wearing surface for the piston rings.

## How it fails

Rarely, and usually as a symptom of something else. Overheating badly enough can distort the deck face so the <Part id="powertrain.head_gasket" /> can no longer seal. A dropped valve or a broken rod can punch through a cylinder wall. Coolant left unchanged for years can corrode the water jacket from the inside.

## How it is serviced

It is not, in normal use. The block is the part everything else is replaced around. When it is genuinely damaged the economic answer is a replacement engine, not a repair.
