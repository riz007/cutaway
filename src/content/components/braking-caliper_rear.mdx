Smaller than its front counterpart, because the rear axle does far less braking, and usually carrying the parking brake mechanism as well.

## What it does

It clamps the <Part id="braking.rotor_rear" /> in the same way the front caliper clamps its rotor, but with less piston area and a smaller effective radius, because weight transfer under braking unloads the rear axle. Applying too much rear braking would lock the rear wheels and cause the car to spin, so the hydraulic split is deliberately biased forward and the <Part id="braking.abs_modulator" /> trims it further.

The complication is the parking brake. A rear caliper must also apply mechanically and stay applied with no hydraulic pressure, so it contains a screw mechanism that winds the piston out — driven by a cable, or by the <Part id="braking.epb_actuator" /> on electric systems.

That screw mechanism is why a rear caliper piston must be wound in rather than simply pushed back when fitting new pads.

## How it fails

Seizure, either of the guide pins or of the parking brake screw thread, which leaves the brake dragging. Rear calipers seize more often than fronts because they run cooler and see less use.

## How it is serviced

Guide pins cleaned and greased at each pad change.
