A torque converter lets an engine idle while the car stands still in gear, and it multiplies torque while pulling away. It does both with no friction surfaces at all — just oil.

## What it does

Three bladed wheels sit in a sealed doughnut of fluid. The impeller is driven by the engine and flings oil outward. The turbine, connected to the gearbox input, is pushed round by that oil. Between them sits the stator.

The stator is what makes it a converter rather than just a coupling. Oil leaving the turbine is travelling backwards relative to the impeller; the stator redirects it so it re-enters the impeller helpfully rather than fighting it. That redirection is a reaction force, and it means the turbine can output more torque than the engine put in — up to about twice as much at stall.

As the turbine catches up with the impeller, the oil's angle changes, the stator's one-way clutch lets it freewheel, and the unit becomes a plain fluid coupling. At that point a lock-up clutch closes to remove the remaining slip, because slip is lost fuel.

<Note>
Slip is largest at a standstill and falls as speeds converge. That is why an automatic creeps forward on idle and why it feels most eager just off the line.
</Note>

## How it fails

Lock-up clutch material wears and contaminates the fluid; the stator one-way clutch can seize, which ruins economy, or slip, which ruins acceleration.

## How it is serviced

Through transmission fluid changes. Burnt-smelling fluid or dark debris is the early warning.
