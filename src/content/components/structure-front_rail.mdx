The main longitudinal member running from the front bumper back into the floor. In a frontal impact it is the primary load path.

## What it does

Two things in normal use: it carries the <Part id="suspension.subframe_front" /> and the powertrain, and it ties the front of the car to the passenger cell.

In a crash it becomes the most important structure in the vehicle. It is engineered to collapse progressively from front to back, folding like a concertina at a controlled force so that deceleration is spread over as much distance as possible. Beads, holes and changes of section along its length are trigger points, placed to make sure it folds where the engineer intended rather than buckling sideways.

Its rearward section is deliberately much stronger, so the collapse stops before reaching the cabin and the remaining energy is diverted around the passenger cell through the sills and the floor tunnel.

Height matters too. Rails are set at a common height across the industry so vehicles engage each other's structure in a collision rather than one riding over the other.

## How it fails

Only by doing its job, or by corrosion.

## How it is serviced

Never. After a frontal impact, rail alignment is measured on a jig; a deformed rail is replaced or sectioned, never heated and pulled straight, because that destroys the steel's engineered properties.
