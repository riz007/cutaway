The MacPherson strut is the dominant front suspension design on transverse front-wheel-drive cars, and its appeal is packaging: it does three jobs in one column and leaves the engine bay clear.

## What it does

The strut combines the damper, the coil spring around it, and the upper steering pivot. Its lower end bolts to the hub carrier; its upper end mounts to the body through a bearing that lets the whole assembly rotate as the wheel steers. Lateral location comes from the <Part id="suspension.control_arm_lower" /> below.

Because the strut is a structural member and not just a damper, its tube carries bending loads that a conventional damper never sees. That is why strut bodies are so much heavier than the dampers in a wishbone setup.

The compromise is geometric. As the wheel moves up, camber changes in a way a double wishbone can control much better. For a road car at road speeds it hardly matters, which is why the design has lasted since the 1950s.

<Note>
The spring and the damper do different things. The spring decides how far the wheel moves for a given bump; the damper decides how quickly it stops moving afterwards. A worn damper does not lower the car, but it lets it keep bouncing.
</Note>

## How it fails

Damper seals leak and damping fades, top mount bearings wear and make the steering notchy, and spring seats corrode.

## How it is serviced

Dampers are inspected for leaks and replaced in axle pairs. Wheel alignment is checked after any strut work.
