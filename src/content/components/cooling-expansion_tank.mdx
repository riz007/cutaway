Coolant expands as it heats — several percent by volume across the operating range. The expansion tank gives it somewhere to go, and holds the pressure cap that keeps the whole system above atmospheric pressure.

## What it does

Two jobs. First, it accommodates thermal expansion, so the system can be completely full of liquid when hot without bursting a hose. Second, its cap contains a spring-loaded valve that holds roughly one bar of pressure, raising the coolant's boiling point well above 100 degrees Celsius.

A second, smaller valve in the cap lets air back in as the system cools and the coolant contracts. Without it the hoses would collapse.

The tank is also the diagnostic window into the system. The level between the marks, the colour of the fluid, and whether bubbles appear at idle all tell you something.

<Warning>
Pressurised and hot. Removing the cap on a warm engine releases the pressure that was holding the coolant below its boiling point, and it will boil over instantly.
</Warning>

## How it fails

Caps lose their calibrated pressure long before they look worn, which lowers the boiling point and causes overheating that no other component explains. Plastic tanks craze and split at moulding seams.

## How it is serviced

Check the cold level at every service. Replace the cap whenever the cooling system is being worked on — it is a cheap part with an outsized effect.
