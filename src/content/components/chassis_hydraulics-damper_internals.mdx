A piston on a rod moving through oil in a sealed tube, forcing that oil through small valved orifices. This is what damping physically is.

## What it does

The spring decides how far the wheel moves; the damper decides how quickly it stops moving. Without damping, a car struck by a bump would oscillate on its springs for many cycles, and the tyre would spend much of that time only lightly loaded — which is a grip problem, not just a comfort one.

The damper converts that oscillation into heat. Forcing oil through a restriction takes work, and that work comes out of the suspension's energy.

Crucially it is velocity-sensitive, not position-sensitive. Force rises with how fast the piston moves, so the damper barely resists slow body roll while strongly resisting the fast movement of a sharp bump.

Its valving is asymmetric by design: typically soft in compression, so a bump is absorbed, and firm in rebound, so the wheel is not thrown back out. Multi-stage shim stacks give a different rate at low and high piston speeds, letting a car be composed over undulations and still supple over sharp edges.

## How it fails

Seal wear letting oil out, and internal wear. A worn damper gives float, nose dive and poor tyre contact long before it visibly leaks.

## How it is serviced

Inspected for leaks; replaced in axle pairs.
